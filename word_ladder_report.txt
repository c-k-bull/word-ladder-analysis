1. SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The most useful habit for a person solving a word ladder is to watch the
consonant/vowel pattern. Pairs whose patterns differ need far longer detours,
and a simulated solver that fixes the vowel pattern first solves more puzzles
with fewer wasted steps. Working from both ends at once is the second most
useful habit; it is the biggest single boost to finishing a puzzle.

The study tested three hypotheses on 1,095,747 noun pairs of 3, 4 and 5
letters, then compared computer search algorithms and a simulated human
solver.

  Hypothesis                          Verdict        Key evidence
  ----------------------------------  -------------  ---------------------------
  1. Bridge letters will be the       Partly true    Raw top seven: a, e, s, t,
     common ones (r, s, t, l, n, e)                  l, r, n. Relative to how
                                                     common each letter is,
                                                     b, d, s, f, c, t are
                                                     over-used and vowels
                                                     (especially e) under-used.

  2. More letters in common means     True, but      Each differing letter needs
     a shorter ladder                 partly by      at least one step. The
                                      definition     extra detour also shrinks:
                                                     4-letter pairs average 2.3
                                                     extra steps with 0 matches
                                                     vs 1.0 with 2. Letters
                                                     shared in DIFFERENT
                                                     positions make ladders
                                                     longer.

  3. Similar vowel/consonant          Strongly true  With length and differing
     patterns mean shorter ladders                   letters held fixed, 4-letter
                                                     pairs average 1.6 extra
                                                     steps with matching
                                                     patterns vs 5.7 with every
                                                     position mismatched.

For computers, plain breadth-first search is the best tool: always shortest,
and the fastest in practice on graphs this small. Guided searches do less work
but often miss the shortest ladder. A* always finds it with 3-4x less searching
than plain breadth-first search, but is no faster in wall-clock time.

For people, simple rules matter enormously. A simulated solver choosing at
random solves 6% of puzzles; one that always moves toward the target solves
73%; adding vowel-pattern and both-ends rules raises this to 78% overall, and
to 99-100% for 3- and 4-letter puzzles.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. GOALS AND APPROACH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The goal is practical: find patterns a person can use to solve word ladders,
without running a full search in their head.

Questions asked:

  - Which letters get used as temporary "bridge" letters, when they appear in
    neither the start nor the end word?
  - Does sharing more letters make a ladder shorter?
  - Do matching consonant/vowel patterns (for example CCVCV and CCVCV) make a
    ladder shorter, beyond the letters shared?
  - What are the most common changes in a shortest ladder? Is it best to match
    vowels early, match the first and last letters early, or use common
    letters?

The work ran in five stages:

  1. Build the puzzle set. A list of common singular nouns of 3, 4 and 5
     letters, so every puzzle uses familiar words.
  2. Solve every pair exactly. A C++ program found every shortest ladder for
     every same-length pair.
  3. Describe shortest ladders. A Python analysis tested the hypotheses and
     labelled every move.
  4. Benchmark computer strategies. Rules suggested by stage 3 were turned
     into search strategies, timed, and checked for whether the first ladder
     found was a shortest one.
  5. Simulate a human solver. A model of a person with one-step lookahead,
     limited memory and limited patience tested the same rules as practical
     habits.

3. DATA AND METHODS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All results use 2,382 common singular nouns as puzzle words and 5,178 common
words as allowed in-between steps.

Word lists

  Common words (5,178): words of 3-5 letters from the ENABLE Scrabble
  dictionary (no proper nouns or abbreviations), kept if their frequency in
  everyday English is at least about one per million words (wordfreq Zipf
  score >= 3.0), with profanity and slurs removed.

  Puzzle nouns (2,382): the common words used mainly as nouns, plus words with
  a frequent noun sense (such as "walk" and "play"), judged with WordNet.
  Plurals and -ing verb forms were removed, and each length was trimmed to an
  even count: 354 three-letter, 936 four-letter and 1,092 five-letter nouns.

Pairs and ladders

  Every unordered pair of same-length nouns was solved. A breadth-first search
  recorded every shortest ladder for each pair, up to 1,000 per pair (one pair
  exceeded this).

  Length      Pairs     No ladder   Mean steps   Mean detour   Need a detour
  ---------   -------   ---------   ----------   -----------   -------------
  3 letters    62,481      5.0%        3.67         0.93           52.1%
  4 letters   437,580     14.6%        5.69         2.07           85.0%
  5 letters   595,686     76.3%       13.18         8.71           97.2%

Definitions

  Steps                Letter changes in a shortest ladder.
  Differing positions  Positions where the start and end letters differ. A
                       ladder needs at least this many steps.
  Detour               Steps minus differing positions: the extra steps forced
                       by missing words.
  Bridge letter        A letter that appears in an in-between word but in
                       neither the start nor the end word.
  Vowels               a, e, i, o, u, and y when it is not the first letter
                       and is not followed by a vowel ("sky" and "gym" count;
                       "yarn" does not).

Weighting

  Each pair counts equally: a pair with 9 shortest ladders gives each ladder
  weight 1/9. Results are direction-free, since any ladder also works in
  reverse.

4. HYPOTHESIS 1: COMMON LETTERS ARE THE BRIDGE LETTERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Verdict: PARTLY TRUE. The raw ranking matches the prediction, but mostly
because those letters are common everywhere. Measured against how often each
letter appears in the dictionary, the over-used bridges are consonants and the
under-used ones are vowels.

Raw ranking. By the share of ladders that use a letter as a bridge (all
lengths pooled), the top seven are:

  a 33%   e 29%   s 28%   t 28%   l 24%   r 20%   n 20%

That is almost exactly the predicted r, s, t, l, n, e, plus a.

Fairer test. Each letter's share of all bridge-letter appearances, divided by
its share of letters in the dictionary. A ratio above 1.0 means the letter is
used as a bridge more than its commonness predicts.

  Letter  Ratio   Letter  Ratio   Letter  Ratio   Letter  Ratio
  ------  -----   ------  -----   ------  -----   ------  -----
  b       1.89    m       1.20    i  (V)  0.83    e  (V)  0.46
  d       1.57    n       1.08    g       0.59    v       0.44
  s       1.53    l       1.04    o  (V)  0.58    j       0.33
  f       1.45    r       1.00    z       0.51    x       0.16
  c       1.42    w       0.89    u  (V)  0.50    q       0.00
  t       1.33    y       0.84
  p       1.27    a  (V)  0.83
  k       1.24
  h       1.22                                       (V) = vowel

  - Over-used: b, d, s, f, c, t (1.3-1.9x). These are consonants that start or
    end many short words, so swapping one in opens new routes.
  - Under-used: e (0.46x), u, v, g, o (about 0.5-0.6x), a and i (0.8x).
    Vowels change often, but usually to the vowel the end word needs, not to
    a temporary one.
  - Almost never used: j (0.3x), x (0.16x), q (never).

One quirk of the word lists inflates s. The puzzle words contain no plurals,
but the in-between words do, so plural forms such as "cats" often serve as
stepping stones. The letter e is the opposite case: it is so often in the
start or end word that it rarely counts as a bridge.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. HYPOTHESIS 2: MORE LETTERS IN COMMON, SHORTER LADDER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Verdict: TRUE for letters in the same position; each extra match shortens the
ladder, both directly and by reducing detours. FALSE for letters shared in
different positions, which make ladders longer.

Part of this is guaranteed. If two words differ in 3 positions, the ladder
needs at least 3 steps, so more matches always lowers the minimum. The
informative measure is the detour: the extra steps beyond that minimum. The
detour also falls as matches rise, at every length.

  Mean detour (extra steps) by letters already in the same position

  Matching positions     0       1       2       3       4
  ------------------   -----   -----   -----   -----   -----
  3 letters             1.08    0.51    0.00     -       -
  4 letters             2.30    1.69    1.00    0.00     -
  5 letters             9.43    8.49    6.10    3.36    0.00

  Share of pairs needing any detour

  Matching positions     0       1       2       3       4
  ------------------   -----   -----   -----   -----   -----
  3 letters              59%     34%      0%     -       -
  4 letters              91%     78%     51%      0%     -
  5 letters             100%     98%     90%     64%      0%

Words one letter apart never need a detour, since they are already neighbors.

Letters shared in the wrong position hurt. Holding length and differing
positions fixed, each letter shared in a different position adds detour:

  4-letter pairs, all 4 positions differ    Mean detour   Need a detour
  ---------------------------------------   -----------   -------------
  No letters shared anywhere                    2.13           88%
  1 letter shared, different position           2.48           93%
  2 letters shared, different positions         2.66           96%
  3 letters shared, different positions         2.86           97%

Anagram-like pairs are harder, not easier. A likely reason: a letter that sits
in the wrong position must be moved, and a ladder cannot move a letter, only
replace it. Shared letters in the wrong place also tend to mean mismatched
vowel patterns (Hypothesis 3).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. HYPOTHESIS 3: SIMILAR VOWEL/CONSONANT PATTERNS, SHORTER LADDER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Verdict: STRONGLY TRUE, and the clearest result of the study. With word length
and differing positions held fixed, every position where the consonant/vowel
pattern differs adds detour, and the cost grows steeply after the first
mismatch.

The test compares pairs that differ in the same number of positions, so shared
letters cannot explain the effect. The outcome is the detour.

  Mean detour by number of consonant/vowel pattern mismatches

  Pairs                    0       1       2       3       4       5
  --------------------   -----   -----   -----   -----   -----   -----
  3 letters, 2 differ     0.29    0.51    2.41     -       -       -
  3 letters, 3 differ     0.38    0.63    2.19    2.50     -       -
  4 letters, 2 differ     0.80    1.16    3.05     -       -       -
  4 letters, 3 differ     1.25    1.47    2.88    4.35     -       -
  4 letters, 4 differ     1.60    1.65    2.72    3.85    5.74     -
  5 letters, 4 differ     5.33    7.16   10.29   11.35   12.94     -
  5 letters, 5 differ     5.55    7.22    8.78   10.65   12.01   12.64

One mismatch costs little; two or more cost a lot. A single mismatch is often
fixable in one move (for example a vowel becoming a consonant in place). Two
mismatches often mean a vowel has to shift position, and a ladder can only do
that by passing through words that fit both shapes.

This also explains part of Hypothesis 2: letters shared in the wrong positions
usually come with mismatched patterns.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. ANATOMY OF SHORTEST LADDERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Shortest ladders share a recognizable shape: matching vowels stay put, the
outer consonants do the temporary work, and the structural vowel/consonant
changes happen in the middle of the ladder.

Every move in every shortest ladder was labelled as DIRECT (start letter
straight to end letter) or DETOUR (any move involving a temporary letter).

7.1 Matching vowels are almost never broken

  Position that already matches     3 letters    4 letters
  -------------------------------   ---------    ---------
  2nd letter, vowel                    0.1%         3.9%
  1st letter, consonant                0.9%        22.9%
  Last letter, consonant               8.5%        17.4%

  (Share of ladders that temporarily change that letter.)

7.2 The outer letters are the workhorses

  In 4-letter ladders, the first and last positions each take about 18-20% of
  all detour moves, versus about 13% for each middle position. In 5-letter
  ladders the first letter alone takes 22%.

7.3 Ladders have a shape in time

  Detour moves cluster near the two ends of a ladder: step away from the start
  word early, step back toward the end word late. Vowel-to-consonant changes
  cluster in the middle. A direction-free "centrality" score (0 = first or last
  move, 1 = exact middle) averages 0.47 for vowel/consonant changes in 4-letter
  ladders, versus 0.36 for consonant-to-consonant swaps and for detour moves.

7.4 The most common swaps are vowel for vowel

  a<->o, a<->e, a<->i and e<->o lead at every length, each 2-4% of all moves.
  Next come vowel<->consonant swaps such as e<->l and e<->t, then consonant
  swaps: d<->t in 4-letter ladders, and l<->r, p<->s and r<->s in 5-letter
  ladders.

7.5 Some letters make words nearly unreachable

  In 4-letter pairs, 59% of pairs with an x have no ladder at all, and 34%
  with a j, versus about 11% for pairs with p, t or b. At 5 letters, pairs
  with a q are unreachable 96% of the time.

7.6 Answers to the original strategy questions

  - Match vowels early? Get the vowel PATTERN right, then protect matching
    vowels.
  - Match the first/last letters early? No. They are the letters detours
    borrow.
  - Use common letters? Yes, but common CONSONANTS as temporary letters, not
    vowels.

8. COMPUTER SOLVERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Plain breadth-first search (BFS) is the best computer method for these
puzzles: always shortest, and the fastest in wall-clock time. Strategy rules
make guided searches do much less work, but they often miss the shortest
ladder.

8.1 Algorithms tested

  First-hit search   Expands words one at a time, skips any word already seen,
                     and stops the moment it reaches the end word. With no
                     strategy this is plain BFS, which is guaranteed to find a
                     shortest ladder. With a strategy it becomes best-first:
                     it expands whichever word the strategy scores best.

  Branch and bound   A depth-first search that records its first complete
                     ladder, then keeps looking and cuts any branch that cannot
                     beat the best so far. It always ends with a shortest
                     ladder.

  A*                 Expands words in order of steps taken plus letters still
                     wrong. Because that estimate never overstates the steps
                     left, the first ladder it finds is always shortest.
                     Strategies only break ties.

8.2 Strategies (each a score built from the ladder analysis)

  G  fewer letters wrong            P  protect matching vowels
  V  vowel pattern                  O  temporary letters in outer positions
  E  ends first                     L  common temporary letters

8.3 First-hit search, 300 pairs per length

  Strategy           First ladder is shortest     Words expanded   Time
                     (3 / 4 / 5 letters)          (all lengths)    (all)
  ----------------   --------------------------   --------------   ------
  none (plain BFS)   100 / 100 / 100%                  454          21 us
  G                   89 /  61 /  34%                   70          16 us
  GV                  79 /  47 /  27%                   56          15 us
  O                   91 /  66 /  39%                  150          28 us
  E                   77 /  45 /  46%                  155          27 us
  P                  100 /  99 /  93%                  425          60 us
  V                   57 /  30 /  24%                   84          24 us

  - Greedy progress (G) is the best single rule: 6x less work than BFS, but
    accuracy falls steeply with word length, because long ladders need detours
    and greedy search avoids them.
  - Less work did not mean much faster. A guided search keeps a sorted priority
    queue, which costs more per word than BFS's simple queue; at 5 letters G is
    slower than BFS (35 vs 31 us).
  - Ends-first (E) is the most accurate guided rule at 5 letters (46%),
    probably because it is less greedy about the middle letters.
  - Vowels-first (V) alone is the least accurate; it ignores consonants.
  - P behaves almost like plain BFS, because most words tie on it.

8.4 Branch and bound

  Every final ladder was shortest, with no search hitting the expansion cap.

  Strategy   First ladder is shortest   Time per puzzle (3 / 4 / 5 letters)
  --------   ------------------------   -----------------------------------
  none                 1%                 1.3 ms /  8.3 ms / 13.7 ms
  G                   31%                 1.3 ms /  2.5 ms / 22.1 ms
  GV                  33%                 0.8 ms /  0.9 ms / 21.8 ms
  GVP                 33%                 0.8 ms /  0.9 ms / 21.8 ms

  - GV and GVP (identical) were the best combinations: about 9x faster than
    plain branch and bound at 4 letters.
  - At 5 letters plain branch and bound was fastest, because re-sorting
    neighbors costs more than it saves when the graph is this thin.
  - The plain version's first ladders are enormous (about 400 extra steps on
    average), because depth-first search wanders before it improves.
  - Even at its best, branch and bound was at least 35x slower than BFS, and
    usually hundreds of times slower.

8.5 A* (separate 300-pair test run)

  Length   BFS words   A* words   BFS time   A* time
  ------   ---------   --------   --------   -------
  3            137         39       9 us      10 us
  4            525        115      22 us      21 us
  5            756        462      26 us      62 us

  A* found a shortest ladder in all 11,700 test searches. It searched 3-4x
  fewer words at 3 and 4 letters but was no faster, and twice as slow at 5
  letters, where "letters still wrong" underestimates the long detours.

8.6 What carried over

  Rules built from the description of shortest ladders do not make good
  shortcuts on their own; they help only when combined with plain progress (G).

9. HUMAN-LIKE SOLVER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Simple rules matter enormously for a person: random choices solve 6% of
puzzles, always moving toward the target solves 73%, and the full rule set with
vowel-pattern and both-ends rules solves 78%, including 99-100% of 3- and
4-letter puzzles.

9.1 The simulated person

  - Looks only one step ahead: the words one letter away from the current word.
  - Picks the best option by their rules, breaking ties at random.
  - Never reuses a word already in the ladder, and remembers dead ends.
  - When no option is left, erases the last word and tries again.
  - Gives up after 60 moves (each word written or erased) or 15 erasures.

9.2 Rules (combined by letters)

  G  Make progress: change a letter to its target letter; otherwise a sideways
     change; breaking a correct letter last.
  V  Fix the vowel pattern first.
  E  Fix the first and last letters first.
  P  Never change a matching vowel.
  O  Detour through the first or last letter.
  L  Detour through common letters.
  B  Build from both ends at once, each side aiming at the other's latest word.

9.3 Results, 500 puzzles per length, 3 attempts each

  Differences under about 2 points are within noise.

  Rules      Solved            Solved with a shortest   Extra steps when
             (3 / 4 / 5)       ladder (3 / 4 / 5)       solved (3 / 4 / 5)
  --------   ---------------   ----------------------   ------------------
  none        9 /  3 /  5%     0.3 / 0.2 / 0.5%         28  / 25  / 12
  G          95 / 92 / 32%      57 /  24 /   5%         4.0 / 8.7 / 10.0
  GE         95 / 90 / 32%      58 /  25 /   5%         3.9 / 8.2 / 10.9
  GP         96 / 91 / 33%      59 /  26 /   6%         3.7 / 8.1 / 10.1
  GO         92 / 87 / 32%      56 /  26 /   5%         3.7 / 8.1 / 10.5
  GL         93 / 87 / 35%      60 /  27 /   5%         4.0 / 7.7 / 11.4
  GV         98 / 94 / 38%      62 /  28 /   6%         2.8 / 7.0 / 11.4
  GB         99 / 96 / 34%      59 /  26 /   6%         3.6 / 8.6 / 10.9
  GVPOLB    100 / 99 / 36%      64 /  26 /   5%         2.0 / 7.2 /  9.9

  - Vowel pattern (V) is the most helpful single addition. It solves more
    puzzles at every length and cuts wasted steps by about 20-30% for 3- and
    4-letter puzzles.
  - Both ends (B) is the biggest boost to finishing. 3-letter puzzles go from
    95% to 99% solved and 4-letter from 92% to 96%, with fewer moves. It does
    not make ladders shorter.
  - Detour rules help only in the full set. O and L alone slightly lower the
    solve rate for 3- and 4-letter puzzles, but GVPOLB, which includes them,
    is the best rule set for those lengths.
  - Protect vowels (P) and ends first (E) are neutral. Making progress already
    avoids breaking a matching letter, so P adds nothing.
  - 5-letter puzzles are hard for everyone: about 35% solved within 60 moves.
    Their shortest ladders average 13 steps, and one-step lookahead may be too
    weak for them.

The simulated person usually finishes but rarely with a shortest ladder (64% at
best for 3 letters, 26-28% for 4). The model wanders sideways rather than
erasing, and it never tidies its ladder at the end the way a person reviewing
their work would.

10. HOW TO SOLVE A WORD LADDER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The evidence points to a short routine: read the vowel pattern, build from both
ends, fix letters when you can, and detour through an outer consonant when you
can't.

  1. Compare the vowel patterns first. Write each word as consonants and vowels
     ("cold" is CVCC, "warm" is CVCC). If they match, expect a direct ladder.
     If two or more positions differ, expect a detour and plan where the vowel
     will shift.
  2. Work from both ends. Extend from the start and the end in turn, aiming
     each side at the other's latest word, until they are one letter apart.
  3. Fix a letter whenever you can. Change a letter to its final target letter
     if that makes a real word. Prefer fixes that bring the vowel pattern into
     line.
  4. Leave a matching vowel alone. If the start and end share a vowel in the
     same spot, keep it.
  5. When stuck, borrow an outer consonant. Swap the first or last letter for
     a common consonant (b, d, s, t, c, p) to open new words, then swap it to
     the target letter later.
  6. Don't lock the first and last letters early. Those are the letters
     detours need.
  7. Expect trouble from anagrams and rare letters. Pairs that share letters
     in different positions take longer, and words with x, j, q or z are often
     unreachable.
  8. Tidy up at the end. If two words that aren't neighbors in your ladder
     differ by one letter, cut out everything between them.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The 3- and 4-letter results are solid; the 5-letter results and the human model
are the weakest parts and the best places to extend the work.

  - The 5-letter graph is thin. With common words as in-between steps, 76% of
    5-letter pairs have no ladder and the rest average 13 steps. Rerunning with
    the full Scrabble list as in-between words would show whether the 5-letter
    findings hold when most pairs connect.
  - Results describe these word lists. A different vocabulary or noun filter
    would shift the numbers, though the direction of the main effects is
    consistent across lengths.
  - Strategy timings are machine-specific. Words expanded is the more reliable
    comparison; times in microseconds vary with hardware and implementation.
  - The human model is simple. It has perfect vocabulary by default, one-step
    lookahead, and never tidies its ladder. Useful extensions: a final tidy-up
    step, a smaller vocabulary (--vocab 0.7), two-step lookahead, and
    validation against real people solving the same puzzles.
  - Sample sizes. The strategy benchmarks used 300 puzzles per length and the
    human simulation 500 per length with 3 attempts each. Differences of a
    point or two are within noise.
